Now an iconic view of the city, the pier head was Liverpool's dockland heart for hundreds of years. Prior to the construction of the modern docks from 1715 onwards, vessels would lay anchor in the estruary and have goods taken back and forth via small boats in a timeconsuming process that was interrupted by tides.

The Steers Dock changed all that by making use of the tidal inlet which had given the city its name, and eventually these more modern enclosed docks spread out from the inlet to cover the pier head area before eventually expanding miles to the north and south. The dock that was built on the site we now know as the Pier Head was George's Dock.

## An Iconic View of Global Significance

Liverpool's three graces were built between 1909 and 1917, and redefined the Liverpool waterfront. They were built on land gained by filling in George's Dock, and moved the city's waterfront hundreds of feet further into the Mersey estruary, leaving the Strand no longer true to its name. Symbols of their era, they represent the golden age of Liverpool's mercantile trade.

## The Royal Liver Building

No longer embroiled in the moral morass of slavery and its cotton, Liverpool had become the city of emmigration. Nine million people are estimated to have left for the Americas from the landing stages along the shores of the city. For many millions, the Liver Birds atop the Royal Liver Building would have been the final view of European civilisation.

The middle of the Three Graces, and the last to be built, was the Cunard Building. It served as headquarters for the famous shipping line for decades, epitomising what the city had become. No doubt it brought pleasure to those in charge that the new building hid the headquarters of their major rival the White Star Line, which stands across the Strand on the corner of James Street. Two of the preemininent shipping lines in the world headquartered just yars apart in Liverpool, where both companies were founded.
